01 / WHY PRAXIS?

The difference between
saying and doing

“A system can appear to work while failing at its purpose.”

An agent reports that a task is complete. Its explanation is coherent. Its actions look plausible. Yet the required outcome may never have occurred. This is a problem of verification: the difference between a convincing account of success and evidence that success actually happened.

Recent research makes this distinction concrete. Gao and Zhou's 2026 paper, Can Agent Benchmarks Support Their Scores?, describes how a benchmark can record a successful action—such as clicking “Save”—without verifying that the intended state change occurred. A success score is only as reliable as the evidence behind it. Read the research paper ↗

The deeper problem is not new. Literature has long examined the distance between appearance and reality, the contradictions embedded in systems of rules, and the power to determine what counts as truth. These ideas offer useful ways to frame the failure modes we need to test in intelligent agents.

Don't evaluate the claim. Evaluate the evidence. Agent Praxis translates these problems into controlled, adversarial experiments. It asks whether an agent's actions satisfy explicit requirements, whether the resulting state supports its claims, and whether apparent success survives independent verification.

02 / THE SIX WORLDS

Six literary worlds.
Six tests of intelligent action.

Literature has long explored the distance between what appears true and what is true, between rules and reason, between intention and consequence. Agent Praxis translates six literary ideas into controlled, adversarial environments. Each begins with a human insight, connects it to a problem in intelligent action, and turns that problem into an experiment.
